Product architecture · internal

Witness reads like a toolbox and looks like a newspaper

Thirty-eight screens, six feature families, one universal destination that could not reach five of them. A map of what talks to what, the screens that talk to nothing, the doors now cut — and two ways to make the whole thing cohere.

7 August 2026 · diagnosis at c0f8a1f · cleanup shipped at 32774c9

01The read

Nothing in Witness is bad. That is what makes the eclectic feeling hard to place. Tree Health is a serious piece of forensic work. The Portrait answers the relational question a tree diagram is usually drawn for. The Query Library counts 329 real questions against your own dead. Each feature would survive on its own.

The problem is not the features. It is that they barely speak to one another. I traced every navigation edge in apps/mobile/src and the shape that came out is a funnel, not a graph: six independent families of screens, all of them pushing into ancestor/[id] — and ancestor/[id] pushing back out only to other people, an era query, and a relationship path. Their place, their household, and their findings had no door at all.

Every road leads to the Portrait, and almost every door out of it led to another person. The features feel unrelated because, structurally, they were.

02Three findings

The Portrait is a sink, not a hub

This is the whole problem in one screen. A user arrives at Patrick Howe from a Tree Health finding, from a NARA draft card, from a map pin, from a "47 of your ancestors were alive" card, from a search. Each of those arrivals carries a reason. The Portrait discards the reason and shows the same register of parents, siblings, and children every time.

Worse, it cannot send you back. From Patrick you cannot reach Patrick's place, Patrick's archive candidates, Patrick's health findings, or Patrick's household. Six feature families can put you in the room; the room knows about none of them. Fixing this one screen would do more for cohesion than any amount of re-shelving, because it is the only screen every feature already agrees on.

The spine specified in the brief was never built

PROJECT_BRIEF.md §Branch Focus / Identity Lens: "User designates a heritage branch… entire product reorients around that lens — queries, historical context, map, notifications." Grep the codebase for branchFocus or branch_focus: zero hits. It does not exist.

Nor do the other cross-cutting features from the same document — The Recognition Feature, Family Constellation, Annual Family Wrapped, Inheritance Transfer, Cemetery GPS, Wikidata, Chronicling America. Look at that list: every unbuilt feature is a connective one, and every shipped feature is a vertical one. The app doesn't feel eclectic by accident. The ribs shipped and the spine was descoped.

Two shelves are competing for the same job

The National Archives screen is on the Explore shelf and the Tree shelf. That duplication is the tell: Explore means "wander" and Tree means "work," NARA is both, so it was filed twice. Meanwhile Ocean Crossings — arguably the most emotionally loaded fact in an American tree — is the fifth card down on the wander shelf, and Tree Health, the app's most serious asset, is at the bottom of the work shelf. Nothing is ranked by how much it matters.

The one thing already doing this right

src/lib/research-ledger.ts aggregates Tree Health, Orphan Records, and the Archives into a single "Your findings · 43 decisions so far" band. It is the only code in the app that treats three features as one thing. It works, it is 200 lines, and it is used on exactly one screen. Both approaches below are, in different ways, arguments for generalizing it.

03The orphans

One screen is a true orphan — zero inbound references anywhere in the source, still compiled into every bundle. The rest are what I'd call curios: reachable by exactly one tap from one shelf, with no path onward and no relationship to anything else in the app.

The curios share a diagnosis. They are all findings — computed facts about the tree — but the app files them as destinations, so each one gets a card on a shelf and a screen of its own. A finding that lives on its own screen can only be found by someone who already went looking for it.

04Who this is for

You asked me to think as the amateur who uses this regularly but not as their main tool. That framing does a lot of work, so it's worth being explicit about it.

Their main tool is Ancestry. That is where they edit, attach, and build. They come to Witness on a Sunday afternoon with maybe twenty minutes, having done nothing since last time, and the question in their head is "what's here that I didn't know last week?" — not "let me open the tool that finds kindred couples." They never navigate on purpose. They arrive and expect to be handed something.

Two consequences. First, a shelf of six equal cards is the wrong shape for that visit — it asks them to choose, and choosing requires knowing what each one does. Second, a queue of 443 open Tree Health findings is also the wrong shape, because it's a chore list, and this is a hobby they do for pleasure. Both approaches below are attempts to hand them something instead of asking them to choose.

05Two approaches

Both keep every shipped feature. Neither is a rewrite. They differ in what they make the organizing unit of the app — a finding or a story — and that choice cascades into what Home is, what the Portrait does, and whether the curios survive.

APPROACH A

The Ledger

Witness is a workbench. Every feature produces findings about specific people; findings accumulate in one place; the Portrait shows you everything Witness knows about the person in front of you.

Generalize research-ledger.ts from three sources to all of them. Introduce one typed record — a finding, with a subject person, a kind, a source feature, and a status — and have every feature emit them instead of owning a screen. Tree Health emits findings. NARA emits findings. Kindred, Crossings, Migrations, and the Query Library emit findings. The screens stay, but they become filtered views of one store rather than six unrelated destinations.

What changes

  • The Portrait grows a Findings section under the family register — this person's health flags, archive candidates, kindred marriage, the queries they appear in. The sink becomes a hub.
  • Home leads with the ledger: 17 waiting on you · 43 decided.
  • Kindred and Crossings stop being shelf cards and become finding types. Their screens survive as filters.
  • Every finding carries the reason you arrived, so the Portrait can say "you're here because of a 1917 draft card."

Why it's plausible here

  • The pattern is already proven in your code on three sources, and it reads well.
  • It's mostly server-side — a table and one adapter per feature. Screens change little.
  • It makes Tree Health, your most serious asset, the engine of the app rather than a shelf item.
  • It gives Street View a natural way to land later: a chapter is a finding with a place.

The risk is real and I'd weigh it heavily. A ledger turns a hobby into a chore list. Your live tree already produces 443 Tree Health findings; a home screen that opens with "443 waiting on you" is a scold, and the Sunday-afternoon amateur closes it. If you take this route, the ledger must be capped, ranked, and framed as invitation — which is exactly the discipline the existing ledgerHeading() function already shows you understand.

APPROACH B

The Issue

Witness is a periodical about your family. Each visit is an issue. The features are not tabs the reader chooses between — they are desks that file copy into it.

The strongest argument for this is that you have already built it visually and not structurally. The design system is called Broadsheet. There is a component named Masthead. The type is serif with mono record-text and hairline rules. The app looks like a newspaper and is organized like a toolbox, and I think that mismatch is a large part of the eclectic feeling — the visual language keeps promising an editor's judgment that the information architecture never delivers.

What changes

  • Home becomes this week's issue: a lead story, two or three shorter pieces, a standing column. The digest, featured-today, and Tree-tab curiosities already generate exactly this material.
  • Each feature becomes a desk with an editorial slot — Tree Health files one finding this week, not 443. Archives files the best candidate. Kindred files a piece when it finds something.
  • The Portrait's return path is "next in this issue," which is a far more natural door than "back."
  • The Library becomes the archive of back issues — which is nearly what it is already.
  • Explore stops being a shelf of six equal cards and becomes the index.

Why it fits this user

  • It answers "what's here that I didn't know last week?" directly — that is what a periodical is for.
  • Twenty minutes on a Sunday is an issue-sized visit. A queue is not.
  • Rationing is a feature, not a limitation: one Tree Health finding a week is inviting where 443 is oppressive.
  • It gives Crossings and Kindred a job — they are wonderful stories and poor tools.
  • It makes the subscription legible. You pay for an issue to arrive.

The risk is emptiness. A periodical must publish. A small tree, or a tree the reader hasn't re-imported in six months, may have nothing new — and an issue with no stories is worse than a shelf. This needs an honest answer before you commit: either a deep backlog the editor draws from (you have 329 library queries and 443 findings, so the backlog is real), or an issue that is candid when it's thin.

ALSO CONSIDERED

The Lens — the brief's own answer

The reader picks a branch — a surname, a place, a century — and the entire product reorients around it.

This is Branch Focus from PROJECT_BRIEF.md, and it is the thing the document itself proposed as the spine. I'd argue against reviving it as the spine, for two reasons. It hides data, and an amateur who doesn't understand why the map lost half its pins will read that as a bug. And it fails exactly where it's needed most: a mixed or shallow tree has no dominant branch to detect, so the users with the least cohesive experience get the least help.

But it is an excellent filter on top of either approach above — "show me only the Howes" on a ledger, or "an issue about the Acadian line" — and that's where I'd spend it, once there's a spine for it to sit on.

06What I'd do

A short answer and a longer one.

Short: the cleanup is done — shipped 7 August on fix/cohesion-cleanup at 32774c9. None of it depended on the spine choice, all of it was cheap, and the Portrait change was the single highest-leverage edit in the app.

  • Deleted /ascent and components/ascent/ — dead two weeks, shipping in every bundle.
  • Cut the Portrait's onward doors: the birthplace is now a link to the place, and the family register ends at the household on the stage. Findings stays shut — see below.
  • NARA candidate cards link to the person, and the Archives screen left the Explore shelf for the Tree tab, where its counts already lived.
  • Origins, migrations, crossings, and kindred collapsed into one /patterns entry. The first screen in this app to present several features as one — the dress rehearsal for either spine.
  • Two fixes underneath: stageKeyForPerson() resolves a person to their household (stage keys are the head's id, so a wife's own id silently landed the reader on the root family), and the broadsheet rail keeps Explore lit when you follow a card off its shelf.

One door stayed shut, and it argues for A. The Portrait cannot show this person's Tree Health findings, because findings are computed client-side over the whole tree index on every visit — only the rulings and marks are persisted. Linking them from a person would mean running the full audit on the Portrait, ~11s on an 8.6k tree. That door needs a persisted findings table, which is precisely Approach A's plumbing. It is the strongest concrete argument for building A regardless of which spine wins.

Longer: I'd take B, and use A's plumbing to build it. They're not really exclusive — the Ledger is the data model and the Issue is the framing, and an editor needs a backlog to edit. What decides it is the diagnosis. The app doesn't feel eclectic because it lacks a queue; it feels eclectic because it lacks a subject. Every week's issue is about your family, and that is a subject the toolbox never had.

It also resolves the mismatch you're already living with. You built a newspaper's visual language and then filed everything as tools. Making the structure match the surface is cheaper than the reverse, and you've done most of the hard part already — the digest, the featured-today warmer, and the Tree-tab curiosities are all, functionally, columns waiting for an issue to appear in.
