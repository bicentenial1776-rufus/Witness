# Witness — Onboarding Flow (8 screens → paywall)

Brand voice: quiet confidence, literary not corporate. Playfair Display for headlines, Inter for body. Ink/parchment/amber palette — let amber carry emphasis and CTAs, not decoration.

---

## Screen 1 — Hook

**Visual:** Full-bleed parchment texture, a single faded photograph corner peeking in from the edge, headline in Playfair.

**Headline:**
Somewhere in a box, a drawer, a shoebox of photographs — your family has a story it hasn't told yet.

**Subhead:**
Witness helps you find it.

**CTA:** Continue

## Screen 2 — The problem with what exists

**Headline:**
Names and dates aren't a story.

**Body:**
Most family tree tools give you a chart. A list of who begat whom. Witness gives you *context* — the newspaper clipping, the neighborhood, the world your ancestors actually lived in.

**Visual:** Split screen — left side a sterile tree-chart node (grey, clinical), right side the same name with a warm parchment card around it.

**CTA:** Continue

---

## Screen 3 — How it works, mechanically

**Headline:**
Import what you have. Witness does the rest.

**Body:**
Bring your GEDCOM file — from Ancestry, FamilySearch, or any tree software. Witness cross-references it against historical records, digitized newspapers, and place data to surface what's actually there.

**Visual:** Simple 3-step iconography: Import → Cross-reference → Discover. Keep it literal, not abstract — this screen's job is credibility, not delight.

**CTA:** Continue

---

## Screen 4 — Proof of output (proximity mapping)

**Headline:**
See how close you've always been.

**Body:**
Witness maps where your ancestors lived — and how near they are to you right now. The farm, the tenement, the town your family left. Some of them may be closer than you think.

**Visual:** A map view — a pin for the user's current location, radiating lines out to ancestor locations with distance labels ("14 miles," "1902"). This is your best asset for this screen; let it fill most of the frame.

**Micro-copy (small, bottom):**
You'll see this for your own family in your first week.

**CTA:** Continue

**Optional teaser, small strip at the bottom of this screen or folded into Screen 6/7 — do not give it a full screen of its own:**
*Coming soon: walk the actual street your ancestors lived on, reconstructed in Family Street View.*
Keep this to one line. It should read as a preview of what's ahead, not a promise being made now — nothing you can't already deliver should carry equal visual weight to what you can.

## Screen 6 — Trust: curiosities, not verdicts

**Headline:**
Witness shows you possibilities. You decide what's true.

**Body:**
AI-assisted research means Witness will sometimes surface a match, a record, or a connection that's *worth investigating* — not a fact carved in stone. Every finding is presented as a lead, never a claim. Your family's history stays yours to interpret.

**Visual:** A single example "curiosity card" — soft framing, a small "possible match" label, no false certainty in the design language itself.

**CTA:** Continue

## Notes on sequencing

- Screens 1–4 are pure narrative/credibility — no interaction required, keep swipe-through fast (users should be able to blow through in under 20 seconds if they're impatient).
- The Street View teaser line on Screen 4 is the only mention of an unshipped feature in the whole flow — resist the urge to give it more room. A hard rule worth keeping: nothing labeled "coming soon" should visually outweigh something you can actually deliver in the trial. If it needs more than one line, it's competing with proximity mapping for attention it hasn't earned yet.
- Screen 5 is your only real interaction point pre-trial. Don't add a second one — it dilutes the "this is quick" feeling.
- Screen 6 is doing more strategic work than its length suggests — this is the one screen a skeptical genealogy user (burned by overconfident hint engines elsewhere) needs to see before they'll trust an AI tool with their family history.
- Screens 7 and 8 should feel like one continuous moment, not two separate screens — 7 sets up the ask, 8 (or the paywall itself) makes the mechanics of the trial completely unambiguous. This is where trial apps lose trust if they get cute or vague.
