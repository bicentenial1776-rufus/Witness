The Screens

A person’s page.

The hub of the whole app — every list, card, and pin eventually lands here. One page holds who they were, how they connect to you, what the world was doing around them, and exactly how the record knows it.

How you get here: from anywhere — a digest card, a query result, the family group sheet, a Nearby listing, a search hit, or another person’s register.

1 2 3 4 5
  1. The identity block — the name with its lineage mark; beneath it the record line (woman/man · years · birthplace), then the parentage line.
  2. “Your 2nd great-grandmother ›” — the relationship lede. Tapping it opens How You’re Related: the person-by-person chain from you to them, each step with its own parentage, everyone tappable.
  3. STORY and THEIR WORLD — two panels that open in place. Story writes their biography from the record (noting how many sources it drew from); Their World describes what history was doing around their life. Both are generated once, then kept.
  4. Ancestry › and Share › — the ways off the page: the same person on Ancestry (when the tree came from there), and a share link for family.
  5. The family register — Parents, then Brothers & sisters in birth order with this person highlighted in their place (the ~0 and ~2 beside short lives are ages at death), then each marriage with its children. Every name is a doorway; › marks the tappable ones.

Further down the page: Lived through — the handful of historical events that best frame this life; tapping one opens that event’s results with this person pinned at the top — and How the record knows: the citations, grouped by source, that name this person, with the facts each source supports, page references, and excerpts where the record has them.

A person recorded as living gets a quieter page: “… appears to be living, so Witness keeps their story private.” No biography, no world context, no share link — the register and dates remain.

When the National Archives has candidate records for this person — a draft card, a naturalization — they appear here awaiting your judgment, the same records counted on Explore’s “In the National Archives” card.
