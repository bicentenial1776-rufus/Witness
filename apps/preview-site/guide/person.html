The Screens

A person’s page.

The hub of the whole app — every list, card, and pin eventually lands here. One page holds who they were, how they connect to you, what the world was doing around them, and exactly how the record knows it.

How you get here: from anywhere — a piece of this week’s issue, a digest card, a query result, the family group sheet, a Near me listing, a search hit, or another person’s register.

  1. The issue band — NO. 32 · NEXT: THE TREE CHECK › — appears while you’re reading this week’s issue: the next piece is one tap, however deep the visit has wandered. The × rests it for the sitting.
  2. The identity block — the name with its lineage mark; beneath it the record line (woman/man · years · birthplace), then the parentage line. The birthplace is a doorway — it opens the place itself, everyone who was ever recorded there. On a direct ancestor the line ends with the compass: gen 7 · father’s side — how far back, and which side of you.
  3. “Your 2nd great-grandmother ›” — the relationship lede. Tapping it opens How You’re Related: the person-by-person chain from you to them, each step with its own parentage, everyone tappable.
  4. STORY and THEIR WORLD — two panels that open in place. Story opens with the family chart: two generation bands. Above, the parents inside an amber tie — the marriage — with their own brothers and sisters seated beside them, each on their true side of the family; below, this person’s sibship in birth order, this person lit. Every tile is a name and a birth year; an amber border means that person has a story of their own — tap through to it, while anyone else shows a small fact card, never a dead end. FAMILY CHART · HIDE › puts the diagram away for readers here for the words; the choice is remembered on this device. The story itself weaves these relatives in — where they were, what they were doing — before Their World describes what history was doing around the life. Both are generated once, then kept, and a finished story can be shared or downloaded from the panel. Beside the panels, Research › (open a brief on this person), a link out to the tree’s own provider — Ancestry or FamilySearch, whichever the file came from — and Share ›.
  5. The running head and the register — House of Stephen Gates & Anne Neave · fourth of 13 children: the household and this person’s place in its birth order, before the register itself — Parents, Brothers & sisters with this person highlighted (the ~0 and ~2 beside short lives are ages at death), then each marriage with its children. The register ends with See this household as a length of time ›, which opens their family on the Stage — offered only when the household carries the dated records the Stage needs (a dated marriage, a child with a birth year); its absence means the record is too thin to draw, not that the family is missing.

Further down the page: Lived through — the handful of historical events that best frame this life; tapping one opens that event’s results with this person pinned at the top — and How the record knows: the citations, grouped by source, that name this person, with the facts each source supports, page references, and excerpts where the record has them.

A person recorded as living gets a quieter page: “… appears to be living, so Witness keeps their story private.” No biography, no world context, no share link — the register and dates remain.

Two further sections appear when the record warrants them. From the Tree Check — the audit’s open curiosities naming this person, worded as prompts, not problems; a decision made on the workbench retires them here too. And when the National Archives has candidate records for this person — a draft card, a naturalization — they appear awaiting your judgment, the person’s own slice of the Archives on the Tree tab.
