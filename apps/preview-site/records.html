How Witness handles historical records

Witness compares your tree against historical record sets — ship passenger lists, and record registers such as the rolls of the 1755 Acadian Deportation — and shows you where a record might name one of your ancestors. Three rules govern all of it.

We hold public-domain extracts and links — nothing else

The records Witness carries are facts drawn from public-domain primary sources: government documents, period registers, and books old enough that they belong to everyone. Every row cites the record it came from. We do not scrape, copy, or ingest commercial databases or volunteer transcription projects; where those sites helped us locate a record, we link you to them and say so.

A match is a question, never a verdict

When a name in your tree matches a name in a record, Witness shows you the record, the reasons for the match in plain words, and the source — and asks. Nothing is written to your tree until you decide it is really them, and you can always say it isn't. The same names ran through whole communities; a matching name is the beginning of a question, not proof of descent.

On the Acadian records specifically

The Deportation register begins with the roll of men and boys held at Grand-Pré in 1755, preserved in Colonel John Winslow's own journal — a public-domain record of a painful history. We record only what the roll itself says, exactly as it spelled the names, and we cite both the original manuscript and the transcription that helped us reach it. Wives, daughters, and the other districts are not in that roll; as further public-domain records are added, the register will say where each one came from.
